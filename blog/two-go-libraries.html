I Lost a Benchmark to C++ by 10x, and Shipped Anyway

Two small Go libraries for exchange market data, a bug that was one number wide, and a price that wouldn't sit still.

Every job has a part nobody puts on the poster. In trading systems, it's market data plumbing. Before a single price reaches the clever stuff, someone has to take whatever the exchange throws over the wall and turn it back into numbers. That someone usually has a hex dump open in one window and a 40-page PDF spec in the other.

I've been that someone for a while. This year I pulled two pieces of that plumbing out into open-source Go libraries. fastfix decodes the FAST protocol. lzo1z decompresses LZO1Z. Neither name is winning a branding award. Both do exactly one thing.

This is the story of why they exist, what bit me along the way, and the benchmark I almost didn't publish.

The cgo tax

The usual way to reuse an old C library from Go is cgo. It works. It also means your lovely single static binary now needs a C compiler to build, cross-compiling turns into a hobby, and every call pays a small toll crossing the border between Go and C. When you're calling something on every single packet, small tolls stop being small.

So the plan for both libraries was boring on purpose: pure Go, nothing outside the standard library, go get and you're done.

lzo1z, or: the bug that was one number wide

LZO is a family of compression algorithms from the nineties that refuses to retire, mostly because it decompresses absurdly fast. It comes in flavours. LZO1X is the famous one, and Go already has decoders for it. LZO1Z is the cousin nobody invites to things. It still turns up in a handful of exchange feeds and older embedded protocols.

Here's the fun part. The difference between the two is one constant. A single number inside the decoder is 0x0800 in LZO1X and 0x0700 in LZO1Z. Same format, same everything else. Hand LZO1Z data to an LZO1X decoder and, if you're lucky, you get an error. If you're not, you get bytes that look completely reasonable and are wrong.

Looks-reasonable-but-wrong is my least favourite kind of bug. An error wakes you up. A plausible wrong answer lets you sleep, then ruins your Tuesday.

So the lzo1z README has a section called LZO1Z is not LZO1X, and it's the first thing you see after the install line. I'd rather annoy someone with a warning than have them discover it in production.

A few things I'm quietly pleased with. It never writes past the buffer you give it, so a buffer that's too small gets a clean error instead of corrupted memory. The tests take a real compressed file, chop it off at every possible length, and demand an error from every single stub. And it does all this at around 2 GB/s on a laptop without allocating any memory.

One thing I'm less pleased with, more resigned to: it's GPL. It's a port of the original C library, which is GPL, so the port is too. And because Go builds static binaries, if you import it and ship your program to someone, your program is GPL as well. Running it on your own servers is fine. That warning also sits right at the top of the README, because "surprise, your product is open source now" is not an email anyone wants from Legal.

fastfix, or: the friend who only tells you what changed

FAST stands for FIX Adapted for STreaming. It's an acronym with another acronym inside it, which is very on brand for finance. Lots of exchanges use it to squeeze market data onto the wire.

The idea behind FAST is that it hates repeating itself. If the last message said the price was 22,450.50, the next one might just say "plus five". If a field hasn't changed, it might not mention it at all. Sender and receiver each keep a little notebook of the last values they saw (the spec calls it a dictionary), and every message is basically a list of edits to that notebook.

It's a brilliant way to save bandwidth. It's also a brilliant way to confuse yourself.

The price that drifted

Market data usually arrives over UDP, and most feeds encode each packet on its own, against a blank notebook. That means the decoder has to wipe its notebook at every packet boundary.

Forget, and nothing crashes. The first packet decodes perfectly. The second one applies "plus five" to whatever the previous packet left behind, so the price comes out a little off. The third is a little more off. Nothing fails. The numbers just drift, politely, until someone downstream asks why the order book looks drunk.

In fastfix the fix is one line, dec.Ctx.Reset(), and the README has a section literally titled Context resets — read this one. There's also a test that checks both sides: decode twice without a reset and assert the answer is wrong, then reset and assert it's right. A test that insists on a wrong answer looks odd until you've chased this bug once.

Prices got the same treatment. FAST sends a decimal as a whole number plus an exponent, so 107.15 can arrive as (10715, −2) or as (1071500, −4), and fastfix treats them as the same number. Ask for a price as an integer when it has a fractional part, and you get a "no" rather than a quietly rounded value. Silently rounding a price is worse than refusing it.

Then I benchmarked it, and got humbled

People like numbers, so I measured. Honestly, which turned out to be the important word.

I built one test packet: a market data update carrying ten order-book entries, 174 bytes in all. Then I had every FAST library I could get running decode those exact bytes, wipe the notebook before each packet, and read back every price and size. Each one also had to produce the same checksum or its time didn't count. A fast decoder that gets the wrong answer is just a random number generator with good marketing.

One CPU core, Apple M5, time per packet:

mFAST (C++), generated code       0.27 µs
mFAST (C++), runtime templates    0.67 µs
fastlib (Rust), callback*         1.87 µs
OpenFAST (Java)                   2.41 µs
fastfix (Go)                      2.55 µs
goFAST (Go), hand-written         2.92 µs
fastlib (Rust), structs           4.81 µs
goFAST (Go), reflection           5.22 µs

* Streams values past a callback without building a message, so it does less work than the rest.

The good news: fastfix is the fastest Go FAST decoder I could find, and it gets there without asking you to write code for every message type. It's neck and neck with OpenFAST, the Java reference implementation that a lot of the industry grew up on.

The humbling news: mFAST, a C++ library, is four to ten times faster and allocates no memory per packet. fastfix allocates about 5 KB per packet, because every message becomes a brand new map.

I did think about leaving mFAST out of the README. Then I pictured someone running the benchmark themselves, spotting it, and wondering what else I'd left out. So it's in there, with the reasons.

Those same allocations showed up when I threw more cores at it. With Go's garbage collector on, throughput peaked at four cores and then actually got worse. With the collector switched off for the experiment, it kept climbing to roughly 1.9 million packets a second on ten cores. So the decoders aren't fighting each other. They're just producing a lot of garbage for the collector to clean up. That's the next thing to fix, and now there's a number to beat.

Things I'd tell past me

Pure Go is worth the extra work for anything on the hot path. A build with no C compiler in it keeps paying you back.

Put the dangerous thing at the top of the README. Most people stop reading right after the install command, so that's where the warning goes.

Prefer loud failures. An error is a favour. A plausible wrong number is a loan, and the interest is brutal.

And benchmark against the scary competitor too. Losing on the record beats getting found out later.

Both libraries are on GitHub: fastfix and lzo1z. If you work with exchange feeds and one of them saves you a week, I'd love to hear about it. If one of them costs you a week, I definitely want to hear about it.
